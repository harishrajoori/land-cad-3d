## 3. Directions and facing

### Always use north up in future drawings

```text
                      NORTH / ఉత్తరం
             NW            N            NE
          వాయవ్యం        ఉత్తరం        ఈశాన్యం

WEST       W          HOUSE CENTRE       E       EAST
పడమర     పడమర                         తూర్పు    తూర్పు

             SW            S            SE
          నైఋతి         దక్షిణం         ఆగ్నేయం
                      SOUTH / దక్షిణం
```

This is a direction legend, not a room layout or a prescribed nine-room grid.

| Direction | Telugu | Bearing clockwise from north | Position on a north-up plan |
|---|---|---:|---|
| N | ఉత్తరం | 0° / 360° | Top |
| NE | ఈశాన్యం | 45° | Top right |
| E | తూర్పు | 90° | Right |
| SE | ఆగ్నేయం | 135° | Bottom right |
| S | దక్షిణం | 180° | Bottom |
| SW | నైఋతి | 225° | Bottom left |
| W | పడమర | 270° | Left |
| NW | వాయవ్యం | 315° | Top left |

These are compass bearings, not recommended angles for rotating a door. Mark the survey's north reference; if a magnetic reading is used, record that separately. A casually held phone reading should not silently become a precise site survey.

**Reading the Telugu diagrams:** many illustrations in A use east at the top, north at the left, west at the bottom and south at the right. Read each compass label before interpreting it. Rotate the diagram mentally or redraw it north-up; never copy page positions into the new plan. [A pp. 18, 24–30, 40–72]

### Five different meanings must stay separate

1. **Road frontage:** which boundary touches the road. Property 1 has a west frontage.
2. **Door position:** the location along a wall. A door can be near the north end of the west wall.
3. **Door facing:** the outward direction through the doorway. A door in that west wall can still face west, despite being in the north-west portion of the house.
4. **Person facing:** a cook, worshipper or seated office user faces a direction independently of the room's position.
5. **Sleeping head direction:** where the head rests; it is not the direction a person looks when standing in the room.

For Property 1, someone leaving through an ordinary west-facing door looks west; someone entering through it walks east. This does not make it an east-facing house. The rear boundary is east. For Property 3, the north road is at the top and the rear is south. Property 2 is a west-and-south road corner, not automatically a diagonally south-west-facing entrance.

### Subdirections and reference outlines

The books use expressions such as east of north-east and north of north-east. In boundary descriptions, **ENE** identifies the northern portion of an east frontage; **NNE** identifies the eastern portion of a north frontage. Similarly WNW is toward the north end of a west boundary, WSW its south end, SSE the east end of a south boundary, and SSW its west end. ESE and NNW are the other corresponding ends. [A pp. 40–44, 69–72; B p. 10]

These boundary positions are not automatically the same as a compass bearing sector or a numbered doorway *pada*. For every assessment specify whether the reference is the **plot**, the **building footprint**, a **floor**, or an individual **room**. Draw that outline and its centre before applying a grid. No single entrance-pada numbering method has been established for this project by the supplied material.

## 4. Traditional concepts and terminology

The Telugu books describe Vaastu through the five elements, the Vaastu Purusha narrative, directional guardians, planets, site geometry and surroundings. The narrative places the Purusha's head in the north-east and feet toward the south-west. Fire is associated with the south-east and water prominently with the north-east. These explain the authors' planning preferences within their tradition. [A pp. 13–17, 27–30; C pp. 11–18; B pp. 4–6]

| Direction | Guardian in A | Planet association in A |
|---|---|---|
| NE | Ishwara | Guru and Ketu |
| E | Indra | Sun |
| SE | Agni | Venus |
| S | Yama | Mars |
| SW | Nirriti | Rahu |
| W | Varuna | Saturn |
| NW | Vayu | Moon |
| N | Kubera | Mercury |

This table preserves the book's symbolic framework; it is not a requirement to add deities, symbols or architectural features to each room. [A pp. 27–30]

**Useful Telugu terms:** దిక్కులు — directions; మూలలు — corners; ఈశాన్యం — north-east; ఆగ్నేయం — south-east; నైఋతి — south-west; వాయవ్యం — north-west; పల్లం — lower ground/depression; ఎత్తు — height/elevation; వీధిపోటు — street thrust/focus; ఆయం — traditional dimensional classification. Their meaning depends on whether the passage discusses a plot, wall, room, road or floor.

### Ayam is a separate, unresolved measurement convention

A pp. 22–23 discusses disagreement over whether to measure inside walls, including walls, or using another wall convention. It gives an area-based calculation using dimensions in *gajalu* (yards), multiplication by nine and division by eight, with favourable classifications associated with remainders 1, 3, 5 and 7. A result represented as 8 requires the source's remainder convention, not an unexplained software zero.

Do not apply that formula directly to square feet, silently choose a wall convention, or adjust a house by arbitrary inches to obtain an auspicious number. If the family wants an Ayam calculation, record the chosen tradition, units, dimensions and worked calculation separately with their consultant. The present document does not certify any of the three plot or room dimensions by Ayam.

## 5. Planning knowledge from the supplied books

The following records traditional source preferences. “Proposed application” means a planning interpretation for this family, not a new rule attributed to the books.

### 5.1 Site shape, cuts and extensions

Regular square/rectangular geometry is favoured in B, while A closely distinguishes corner extensions, central projections, recesses, angled boundaries and street approaches. A generally favours a north-east extension and objects to a north-east reduction; this is not blanket approval for every irregular shape. [B pp. 7–10; A pp. 40–57]

**Important for earlier concepts:** A p. 50, figure 1, criticizes a central east-side recess with projecting north-east and south-east arms. Therefore an east-opening C/U-shaped concept must not be described as automatically approved under this book. The earlier concept remains unvalidated. Review the actual outer building and plot outlines, not just room labels.

A's dimensional examples for extensions are contextual examples, not universal ratios. Enlarging a corner, partitioning land, or purchasing adjacent land is not part of the current authorized design work. [A pp. 45–47, 59]

### 5.2 Open space, mass and levels

A repeatedly prefers more openness toward north/east, higher or heavier development toward south/west, a stable south-west, and a relatively unobstructed north-east. It also considers neighbouring buildings, land, roads and water beyond the compound. C stresses that checking a handful of room locations is insufficient. [A pp. 17–19, 30–36, 49–55, 62–72; C pp. 19–20, 25–35; B pp. 10–11]

Keep three separate drawings or annotations: **site ground levels**, **habitable floor levels**, and **building/roof mass**. The source preference for a lower side of a site does not prescribe trip-producing indoor steps, an unsafe plinth, or drainage against the actual available outlet. Proposed application: maintain clear usable indoor circulation and have the engineer reconcile external levels and drainage with the selected traditional preferences.

The books' example gaps, road-width comparisons and wall-height differences are not verified municipal setbacks. They must not become a construction envelope. [A pp. 54, 62, 68–69; B p. 14]

### 5.3 North-east openness has more than one definition

A pp. 38–39 gives two methods for identifying a particularly sensitive corner: subdividing a quarter again (1/16 of the whole), and using a 2 × 2 corner group in a 9 × 9 division (4/81 of the whole). These are different areas. Neither justifies saying the entire north-east quarter must always be vacant.

Proposed application: explicitly mark the selected reference outline and corner zone. Keep it clear of a stair block, heavy storage and a large raised mass. The family's two pooja rooms still need a coherent north-east arrangement; the rule cannot be satisfied by writing “NE” on a room elsewhere. [A pp. 38–39, 47–49]

### 5.4 Roads, gates and entrances

A and B distinguish a road alongside a plot from a road terminating toward it, and distinguish which part of a boundary receives that approach. B p. 10 classifies ENE, NNE, WNW and SSE approaches favourably, with ESE, NNW, WSW and SSW unfavourably. These are the book's street-thrust classifications, not a universal doorway schedule.

For a south-west corner plot, A discusses west/north-west or south/south-east gate options according to surrounding levels and circumstances. It generally prefers one principal gate; where two exist, one remains primary. This pertains especially to Property 2, but does not finalize its entrance without a survey. [A pp. 66–72]

B prefers an unobstructed main entry, discusses east/north-east-related locations, and separately permits a south door with a verandah. Thus one sentence cannot establish that only one facing is allowed. [B pp. 17–18]

Proposed application: annotate the site gate, pedestrian route, ground-floor door and upstairs stair access separately. A parked car must not be the only passage to the main door. For Property 1, retain west-road gates while investigating the owner's selected **north-facing house main door** via a real north-side approach. Do not confuse gate direction with house-door facing or rotate compass zones to reuse another plan.

### 5.5 Kitchen and dining

B recommends the south-east kitchen, with north-west as an alternative; it specifies cooking toward east. It separates the sink from the hob, prefers heavy storage along south/west, and avoids placing the kitchen in the north-east. A also recommends south-east cooking in its site discussion. [B pp. 21–22; A p. 58; C pp. 12, 18]

The family's south-east kitchen remains the design requirement. The book's alternative is not permission to silently relocate it. Locate a practical worktop, sink, hob, refrigerator and clear standing space; draw an arrow for the cook's facing. A kitchen label in the correct corner does not establish that these fit.

B considers the dining relationship to the kitchen, table placement and avoidance of adjacency to a toilet. It discusses multiple dining locations rather than one compulsory zone. The owner's open living–dining requirement can be retained, with a usable route between kitchen and table. [B pp. 20–21]

### 5.6 Bedrooms, office and storage

South-west is the recurring master-bedroom preference. B recommends a sleeping head toward south or east rather than north, and discusses wardrobes and attached bathrooms separately from bed placement. A's south-west discussion also associates this area with sleeping/storage. [B pp. 18–19; A pp. 70–71]

Do not turn “south-west master” into “south-west toilet.” Draw the sleeping area, bathroom and wardrobe as distinct footprints. B allows an attached bath on the west/north side of a bedroom; the whole-building location and the family's pooja separation must still be checked. [B pp. 19–20]

The office replaces the third bedroom. B's study guidance favours an east/north-facing user and addresses light and shelving. Proposed application: examine a north/east-adjacent office location where it does not displace the pooja suite, with adequate privacy for work. This is an option, not a fixed location established by the owner. [B p. 23]

A p. 71 includes a recommendation for a north-east room to be larger than the south-west room in its particular discussion. Therefore this corpus does not support a universal assertion that the master must always be the largest room.

### 5.7 Bathrooms and pooja separation

B discusses bathrooms mainly toward west/south and toilets toward west/north-west, and explicitly discourages north-east attached bathrooms. A discourages a south-west toilet in its corner-site discussion, then mentions a conditional raised arrangement while continuing to object to a south-west pit. These are context-dependent passages, not an instruction to add steps to the family bathroom. [B pp. 19–20; A p. 69]

**The strongest directly relevant passage is B p. 22, item 5:** the pooja room should not share a common toilet wall or be opposite a bathroom/toilet. This directly supports the owner's objection to the earlier adjacency.

Proposed application: put a genuinely usable intervening space between the worship suite and wet rooms; check both wall adjacency and door sightlines. A thin service shaft or a renamed strip should not be used to disguise adjacency. Also review the first-floor bathroom stack above the ground-floor worship rooms; vertical separation is a project review requirement here, not a quotation from that specific item.

### 5.8 Staircase and independence

B p. 25 prefers a stair in the south/west, discourages a heavy stair in north/east, and excludes pooja, safe or toilet immediately beneath it. A also objects to a stair mass in the sensitive north-east. [A pp. 47–49]

For this project the stair must be external to the ground-floor private circulation. Its location is not fixed merely by a south/west label: draw full flights, landings, entrance, upper-floor arrival and usable access from the gate. Do not let it occupy the master sleeping area or block parking and worship access.

B's odd stair-count preference cannot define a safe stair on its own. Floor-to-floor height, equal risers, treads, headroom, landing and railing design must be established by the engineer. The book also gives an even-count preference for entrance approach steps on p. 17; these are different contexts and should not be collapsed into a single count rule.

### 5.9 Water, tanks, wastewater and utilities

A favours north/east-related water locations in multiple examples. B's underground tank discussion similarly favours north/east/north-east, while its overhead-tank guidance is toward west/south-west and avoids the centre. Underground storage and an overhead tank are not interchangeable objects. [A pp. 58, 64, 68–69; B pp. 23–24]

There is a contradictory well-location statement in B p. 13; see the disagreement register. Do not infer a final borewell position from that sentence.

Proposed application: draw potable water, borewell, sump, overhead tank, sewer connection/septic system and stormwater as separate systems. A traditional preference for “water in NE” is not a sanitation design and does not make sewage a permissible substitute for clean water. Exact separations, tank support, pipe slopes, water quality and available discharge points remain engineering questions. No mandatory tank dimensions or platform heights are adopted from these texts.

### 5.10 Parking, garden, light and interiors

B p. 16 favours north-west parking and discusses vehicle orientation. Treat this as a preference to test against the real gate and turning geometry. It does not settle whether one or two cars fit, whether a car can leave without moving a bike, or whether the independent stair remains accessible.

A favours heavier trees toward south/west in its site guidance. B's tree guidance contradicts itself on p. 15. Proposed application: retain the requested garden, distinguish low planting from a large tree, and review shade, roots, utilities and maintenance. No existing tree is to be removed merely because of an isolated passage. [A p. 58; B pp. 15–16]

B pp. 26–28 commonly place heavier furnishings toward south/west and lighter arrangements toward north/east. These are furnishing preferences, not a requirement to fill every zone. Prioritize beds with usable access, chairs that can pull out, wardrobes that open, uncluttered circulation and windows that actually ventilate. Conflicting colour prescriptions are not treated as structural requirements.

## 6. Mallanna and daily pooja

North-east pooja is explicitly preferred in B p. 22 and C p. 12. B discusses deity and worshipper orientation, a small separation between altar and wall, a two-shutter door, and separation from bathrooms. A's north-east chapter discourages heavy raised construction and storage. [A pp. 38–39, 47–49]

The supplied corpus does **not** establish a standard domestic Mallanna room size, mandatory four-pillar spacing, platform height, Patnam dimensions, or the family's exact ritual orientation. The platform, four pillars, four-person capacity and prasadam arrangement are **owner requirements**, not rules discovered in these books.

### Proposed reconciliation for a future design

| Element | Preserve | Resolve in a dimensioned drawing |
|---|---|---|
| Two pooja rooms | Daily and Mallanna rooms remain separate and side by side in the NE area | Their entrances, clear floor space, windows and relationship to the sensitive corner |
| Raised shrine | Platform/table-like surface remains | Explore a localized raised altar rather than elevating the whole NE room; this is a proposal needing family/consultant review |

Do not claim the raised-platform tension is fully resolved just by using the word “light.” The family's actual shrine dimensions and accepted ritual practice must be drawn and reviewed. Neither pooja room should become a passage to a bedroom, toilet or staircase.

### Deity facing and worshipper facing

For an ordinary face-to-face arrangement, a deity placed on the east side and facing west lets the worshipper face east. This is a coherent candidate, not an established Mallanna prescription. If the deity faces east, the worshipper opposite it faces west; both cannot simultaneously face east toward each other. B p. 22 lists alternatives that must be checked geometrically rather than copied as independent labels.

Lamps, incense, offerings and decorations need a cleanable and usable arrangement appropriate to the family's actual practice. Discuss the layout with the family's Mallanna/Oggu pujari for ritual specifics and with the engineer for ventilation, structure and clearances. Reading these books does not make the design a priest-approved shrine.
